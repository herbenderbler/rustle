rustle

A Bluetooth Low Energy library for Linux, in plain Rust. It scans, decodes advertisements, and talks GATT.

There is one crate between your program and the kernel’s sockets. No D-Bus client, no async runtime, no other dependencies. The parsers that read the radio are fuzzed. A bad byte stops the parse.

the radio

        
Bluetooth Low Energy does two things.

        
A BLE device either advertises or holds a connection. Advertising is a broadcast. The device sends a short packet, up to 31 bytes in the original format, on three advertising channels, and repeats it anywhere from 50 times a second to once every ten seconds. Anyone nearby can listen. Nothing is addressed to the listener, and nothing is acknowledged.

        
Each packet carries the sender’s six-byte address. The listening radio also measures how strongly the packet arrived, the RSSI, in dBm. Numbers nearer zero are stronger: −48 is close, −90 is far.

        
A connection is a private conversation. The central, here your computer, answers a connectable advertisement, and the two agree on a timetable and hop together across the other 37 of BLE’s 40 channels. The central asks for values and the peripheral answers.

        
rustle does both halves. HciScanner listens to advertisements. L2capAtt and GattClient make the connection and ask the questions.

      


      

        
the stack

        
The acronyms, from the chip up.

        
The radio is its own chip, the controller. Your computer, the host, steers it through HCI, the Host Controller Interface. HCI is a message format the Bluetooth specification fixes, usually carried over USB or a serial line. The host sends commands, such as “start scanning”. The controller sends back events, such as “I heard this advertisement”. Because every controller speaks it, rustle can drive the one on the board or a USB dongle.

        
Once connected, the two devices talk ATT, the Attribute Protocol. The peripheral keeps a numbered table of small values. Each row has a number, its handle, and a type, its UUID. The central says “read row 3” or “write row 4”. ATT rides on one fixed channel of L2CAP, the layer that shares a connection between protocols.

        
GATT, the Generic Attribute Profile, is how that table is arranged: rows grouped into services, such as Battery, with characteristics inside them, such as Battery Level. A characteristic can also push its value when it changes, a notification, once the central asks for that.

        
Most Linux programs reach all of this through bluetoothd over D-Bus. rustle skips both. It scans on the kernel’s raw HCI socket and talks ATT on an L2CAP socket. The kernel still makes the connection.

      


      

        
the path

        
Four steps, then your loop.

        
A usual BLE stack arrives with a runtime, a D-Bus client, and somebody else’s library. rustle is the short version of that drawing. HciScanner opens a raw HCI socket. read returns one datagram. parse_hci_packet turns an advertising report into an address, an address type, an RSSI, a PDU kind, and the advertising data.

        
The default scan is passive. The controller listens. It transmits nothing. Drop the scanner and the scan stops.

        
start asks for the Bluetooth 5 extended scan first. A controller that rejects it gets the legacy commands instead. A coded-PHY-only scan has no legacy equivalent, so that one fails rather than listening on the wrong PHY.

      


      

        
one event

        
The bytes are from a test, not a brochure.

        
Every read is one HCI event. 04 marks an event packet and 3E an LE Meta event. The next byte is how many parameter bytes follow, and the first of those names the subevent. Subevent 02 is a legacy LE Advertising Report. Extended reports use 0D, and once the extended scan commands are in use, legacy advertisements arrive as 0D reports too. rustle decodes both.

        
The name in the picture is one AD structure. 03 says three bytes follow, 09 says complete local name, 46 53 is FS. RSSI D0 is −48 dBm. 7F would have meant the controller had no reading, and rustle stores that as absent rather than +127.

        
          
src/hci.rs · hci_packet_unwraps_le_advertising_report

          
assert_eq!(reports[0].address, "70:C9:4E:AA:BB:CC");
assert_eq!(reports[0].ad.local_name.as_deref(), Some("FS"));

          
github.com/herbenderbler/rustle

        
        
This is the legacy report shape, the one a controller sends on the legacy fallback. Under the extended commands the same advertisement arrives as a 0D report with a longer header, and decodes to the same fields. A structure that claims more bytes than remain is dropped. A length of zero ends the list. The fields decoded before that point are kept. The parse does not panic.

      


      

        
the address

        
It is stored backwards.

        
HCI puts the six address bytes least-significant first. format_addr_le reverses them into the usual 70:C9:4E:AA:BB:CC form. Connect with that string.

        
The other thing a connection needs is whether the address is random. That is addr_type on the report, not something you can see in the hex of the address itself. Pass addr_type == AddrType::Random as peer_random. The wrong flag makes the kernel look for a device that is not there, and the connect times out.

        
          
src/hci.rs · addr_is_reversed_and_uppercased

          
let addr = [0xcc, 0xbb, 0xaa, 0x4e, 0xc9, 0x70];
assert_eq!(format_addr_le(&addr), "70:C9:4E:AA:BB:CC");

          
least-significant byte first, on the wire

        
      


      

        
two reports

        
An active scan can hear the device twice.

        
Passive was listen-only. Active means the controller answers a scannable advertisement with SCAN_REQ, and the SCAN_RSP, when it arrives, is a second advertising report. A lot of devices put their name only in that second packet.

        
ScanMerger holds the primary until its response arrives or the wait you gave it runs out, then emits one MergedAdv. A longer local name from the response replaces a shorter one. The same length keeps the primary’s. Call flush_stale on the way through, or a device that never answers stays in the hold forever.

        
Filter after the merge. ScanFilter keeps a report when every criterion you set matches. Several entries in one criterion match any of them. An empty criterion is skipped, so the default filter keeps everything.

      


      

        
handles

        
The UUID names the thing. The handle is how you use it.

        
L2capAtt opens the ATT channel. The kernel brings the LE link up. ATT lives on fixed channel 0x0004. The MTU starts at 23 and stays there until exchange_mtu.

        
Handles are not implied by the UUID. They differ from device to device, so you discover the table and then read or subscribe by handle. The ruler is the discovery test’s Generic Access service: Device Name at value handle 0x0003, its CCCD at 0x0004, the service running through 0x0009. next_event returns the value handle, the 0003, not the descriptor you wrote to turn notifications on. The mock gives Device Name a notify flag so the test has a CCCD to find. On a real device Device Name is read, and sometimes write, but never notify.

        
          
src/client.rs · discover_all_walks_service_characteristic_descriptor_tree

          
assert_eq!(ch.characteristic.value_handle, 0x0003);
assert_eq!(ch.descriptors[0].handle, 0x0004);

          
one table, from a mock. the next device will differ.

        
        
SecurityLevel::Low does not ask for pairing. Medium and High wait until the link actually reaches that level, so the kernel has to pair, and a pairing agent has to approve it. Agents come from programs such as bluetoothd, so pairing needs one running.

      


      

        
two layers

        
The parsers run anywhere. The sockets open on Linux.

        
Each module keeps its bytes separate from its socket. hci, att, gatt, the scan merge, the filter, and GattClient build on macOS and on Linux. The fuzzer and a deterministic harness both feed hci and att garbage, on purpose.

        
HciScanner and L2capAtt are declared on every platform. Constructing them anywhere but Linux returns unsupported. GattClient does not know which of those it has. It talks to an AttIo. The tests pass it a list of canned replies, so the client never needs a radio.

        
          
src/client.rs · DEFAULT_ATT_MTU

          
pub const DEFAULT_ATT_MTU: u16 = 23;

          
23 until exchange_mtu. a server that offers less still gets 23.

What rustle returns

        

      

    

    


    
Hardware.

    

      

        
Machine
Linux, with BlueZ sockets in the kernel. Elsewhere the crate builds and the tests run.

        
Radio
A Bluetooth controller that does Low Energy. The one on the board, or a USB dongle.

        
Adapter
One nothing else is scanning. rustle programs the controller directly and turns off a scan already running. Stop bluetoothd, or give rustle a dongle of its own.

      

    

    
Stopping bluetoothd drops whatever else is on that adapter, such as a mouse or headphones, until the daemon starts again.


    
Running it.

    
A passive scan needs CAP_NET_RAW. An active scan also needs CAP_NET_ADMIN, because the kernel treats that scan as a device discovery and rustle ends the discovery on the way out. Run the setcap again after every rebuild.

    
cargo build --example battery
sudo setcap cap_net_raw,cap_net_admin+ep target/debug/examples/battery
target/debug/examples/battery Polar

    
The three examples are scan, battery, and probe. Setup is INSTALL.md, the longer guide is USAGE.md, and the module map is ARCHITECTURE.md.
